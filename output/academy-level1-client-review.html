Independent inspection foundations

Why independent inspection matters

An inspector is an independent witness to a commercial movement. Your job is to establish what happened, how the quantity and quality were determined, and which records support the result. Custody transfer is the point at which responsibility for a cargo changes hands. Because several parties may rely on the same figures, measurements must be repeatable and observations must be reported without favouring any party.

Before attending a job, read the nomination and identify the cargo, location, parties, requested tests, reporting deadlines, agreed measurement basis and limits of your authority. Confirm which industry standard and local procedure apply. If an instruction is unclear, resolve it before treating a measurement as final. A commercially plausible number is not evidence that the work was done correctly.

In the field, record the sequence of events, equipment used, tank or line identity, witnesses, times, raw readings, changes in operating conditions and any protest or exception. Keep raw observations separate from later calculations. If the terminal and vessel figures differ, investigate the inputs systematically; never adjust a reading simply to make the totals agree.

JDL practice: describe the observation, cite its source, state the applicable method, and explain any uncertainty. A clear exception reported promptly is more useful than a tidy but unsupported result.

Scope, standards and professional conduct

The nomination is the first control document. Turn it into a job checklist: movement type, cargo grade, custody-transfer points, tanks and lines, samples and tests, parties to witness the work, agreed standards, reporting form and required times. A request to “check quantity” is incomplete until the measurement basis and included inventories are known. The inspector can verify observations and calculations; the inspector cannot rewrite a contract or certify an unperformed test.

Standards make measurements comparable across locations. Different parties may use different equipment, but a result is defensible when the agreed method, instrument checks, raw observations and calculation chain are known. Record a deviation as a deviation; obtain authorisation for a revised method rather than silently combining two methods.

A useful field log is chronological. Record arrival, induction, opening and closing gauges, transfer changes, samples, witness names and protests. Distinguish what you directly saw from what an operator told you. A report can quote an operator's statement, but it should identify the source. Confidential commercial information belongs in the authorised report, not casual conversation.

Practice task: write a nomination checklist for a fictional cargo that loads from two shore tanks into three vessel tanks. Include starting and ending line-state checks. Name the records needed to compare shore and vessel totals.

Practice case: two parties disagree

At a fictional terminal, the shore report says 1,240 m³ and the vessel report says 1,228 m³. The inspector should first compare measurement times, tank identities, water deductions, temperatures, trim/list entries, line state and rounding. Write an exception log with the evidence for each figure. The 12 m³ difference is a question to investigate, not an amount to distribute between parties without evidence.

Decision exercise: a supervisor asks you to omit a late valve change because the final figures are close. Record the change, its time, the source of the information and whether it could affect the comparison. Professional independence applies even when the omission would be convenient.

Safety planning and Safety Data Sheets

Plan the work before exposure

Inspection work can involve moving machinery, elevated access, flammable vapour, toxic cargo, pressure, weather and simultaneous operations. Begin with the site induction, permit requirements, current risk assessment and emergency arrangements. Identify the safe access route, communication method, isolation requirements and stop-work trigger. If actual conditions differ from the plan, pause and reassess with the responsible site personnel.

Read the Safety Data Sheet for the actual product before handling it. Check the product identity, hazards, first aid, fire response, spill controls, handling and storage, exposure controls, physical properties, incompatibilities and transport information. The sheet informs the risk assessment; it does not replace site controls or competent supervision. A missing or mismatched sheet is a reason to stop and request clarification.

Benzene exposure, hydrogen sulfide and oxygen-deficient atmospheres require particular attention. Do not rely on smell to detect hazardous gas. Use approved monitoring and the site's response plan. Confined-space entry needs a separate authorised system of work, atmospheric testing, attendant and rescue arrangements. An inspector should never enter merely because a tank appears empty.

Static electricity can build during sampling and transfer. Follow the approved bonding, grounding, equipment and operating controls for the product and site. Do not improvise a faster technique when the control is inconvenient. JDL expects a documented stop-work decision whenever a critical safety control is absent.

Hazard controls in detail

Read the SDS as a decision tool. Identification confirms the material and supplier; hazard and ingredient sections describe the exposure concern; first-aid, fire and release sections shape the emergency response; handling and exposure sections guide controls; properties and stability identify conditions and incompatible materials; transport information informs sample dispatch. Some sections may state “no data”, which is an uncertainty to resolve rather than proof of safety.

For a task risk assessment, list the operation, hazard, exposure route, people affected, existing controls, remaining risk and stop condition. Consider access at height, slippery surfaces, moving equipment, vapour at a hatch, pressure in a sample point, lightning and simultaneous transfer operations. Recheck when the tank, product, weather or operating state changes.

Benzene is an exposure concern in some petroleum cargoes. Hydrogen sulfide can incapacitate quickly and smell is unreliable. Use the site's gas monitoring, exclusion and emergency arrangements; no lesson should encourage opening a hatch or entering a space to “confirm” a reading. Confined-space entry is a separate, controlled activity with permit, atmospheric testing, communication, attendant and rescue capability. An inspector outside that authorisation must stay out.

Static control matters when liquid movement, sampling or clothing can create charge. Use approved conductive equipment and site bonding or grounding arrangements. The safe sampling rate and waiting period depend on the cargo and installation; this review course does not invent universal numbers.

Practice task: for a fictional aromatic cargo, identify which SDS entries would change sample container selection, PPE, monitoring and transport packaging.

Do not infer that diesel gloves, respiratory protection or sample bottles suit the new cargo.

SDS reading exercise: locate the sheet's identification and hazard sections, first-aid and fire measures, spill response, handling, exposure controls, stability and transport information. Explain which entry changes your safe work plan. A sheet is useful only when it belongs to the material in front of you.

PPE and respiratory protection

Choose protection from the hazard

PPE is the last layer after elimination, isolation and safe operating controls. Match head, eye, face, hand, foot and body protection to the task, product and site rules. Inspect each item before use; damaged gloves, contaminated clothing or unsuitable footwear can introduce a new risk. A face shield may supplement eye protection, but it does not replace the protection specified by the assessment.

Respiratory equipment is selected by a competent person from a known contaminant, concentration, oxygen status and task duration. A filtering respirator does not supply oxygen. Air-purifying cartridges have contaminant-specific limits and cannot be assumed suitable for unknown or immediately dangerous atmospheres. Supplied-air or self-contained equipment requires training, inspection and a managed response plan.

A tight-fitting mask must be fit tested and checked for a seal each time it is worn. Facial hair or a poor fit can defeat the protection. Record the device, cartridge or cylinder condition, required checks and any limitations in the job assessment. If monitoring or equipment is unavailable, do not substitute a different mask by guesswork.

JDL practice: confirm the protection before opening a tank, handling a sample or entering a controlled area. Where the hazard exceeds the inspector's training or authorisation, withdraw and escalate.

PPE selection and inspection

A hard hat protects against specified impact hazards only when its shell and suspension are sound. Eye protection must fit the splash or particle hazard; a face shield is supplementary where the assessment requires goggles. Glove material must be compatible with the cargo and duration of contact. Safety footwear needs suitable grip and toe protection; contaminated clothing should be handled under the site's decontamination plan. Inspect, clean, replace and store equipment according to its instructions.

Half-mask and full-face filtering respirators cover different areas, but both rely on breathable ambient air, a known contaminant and a suitable filter or cartridge. A full-face unit may also protect the eyes; it does not turn an unknown atmosphere into a safe one. Self-contained breathing apparatus supplies air from a cylinder but requires specialised training, inspection, duration management and a rescue plan. Selection and change-out are tasks for competent personnel.

Before a tight-fitting device is used, confirm the user's training and fit test, inspect seals and valves, check cartridge identity and expiry, and perform the required user seal check. Facial hair, damaged straps or an incorrect cartridge can invalidate protection. Do not use a failed check as a reason to “work quickly”.

Practice task: compare three fictional situations—liquid splash while sealing bottles, known vapour at a controlled sample station, and an unknown atmosphere near a hatch. State which protection decision can be made from the available evidence and which requires withdrawal and further assessment.

Practice case: a mask is available but the hazard is unknown

An inspector finds a half-mask and cartridges in the equipment box, but no current atmosphere test or confirmed vapour identity. The presence of equipment does not establish suitability. Keep the hatch closed, request monitoring and competent selection, and use the site's escalation route. Compare this with a known, assessed exposure where the selected cartridge, fit test, inspection and change schedule are documented.

Review exercise: distinguish protective gloves against contact, eye and face protection against splash, and respiratory protection against inhalation. For each, identify the hazard it controls and the failure mode that would make it ineffective.

Representative and commodity-specific sampling

Define the sample's purpose

A sample is representative only of the material, place and time it actually covers. Before collecting it, confirm the requested test, tank or line, cargo grade, sampling point, method, number of increments and required containers. Clean and inspect equipment. Avoid introducing residues from an earlier product. Record the level, time, temperature where relevant, and operating condition.

An all-levels sample is collected across the liquid depth; a running sample follows a controlled path through the column. Upper, middle and lower spot samples target defined zones and may reveal layering. A composite combines specified increments in agreed proportions. Tap and pipeline samples depend on point condition and whether the line is flowing and homogeneous. Vapour-pressure samples need equipment and handling that limit loss of volatile components. These methods answer different questions and are not interchangeable.

For crude oil, fuel oil, gasoline and clear chemical products, consider viscosity, volatility, water separation, contamination risk and material compatibility. Where an upper, middle or lower position must be calculated, use the verified liquid height and the governing method, then write down the resulting target depths. Do not call a convenience sample representative simply because it looks normal.

JDL practice: connect every sample identifier to the exact source and method. If conditions prevent the agreed method, document the limitation and seek a revised instruction before proceeding.

Sampling methods and their limitations

Equipment choice begins with the sample's purpose. A depth sampler, open bottle, closed system or pipeline point may collect very different material. Check cleanliness, material compatibility, opening and closure, and the sampling point's relation to the cargo. An all-levels or running sample spans a liquid column but can underrepresent layers if the filling rate or path is wrong. Spot samples deliberately describe a defined location. A composite is meaningful only when component increments and proportions are recorded.

For upper, middle and lower positions, first establish the actual product depth above free water. Use the nominated method's defined positions, calculate target gauges, and keep the field readings. If the product depth or roof condition changes, recalculate rather than reusing yesterday's positions. A bottom or dead-bottom sample may answer a contamination question but is not a replacement for a representative product sample.

Tap samples require a sound point and attention to stagnant branch content. Pipeline samples require stable flow and an agreed method for start, middle or end of transfer. Vapour-pressure work is especially vulnerable to evaporation and handling changes; use the specified sealed method and avoid unnecessary transfers between containers.

Commodity decisions differ. Crude oil can contain water, sediment and viscosity gradients; fuel oil may require temperature-aware handling; gasoline loses light components easily; clear chemicals expose small contamination that a darker product might conceal. Sample identity, container material and mixing instructions should follow the requested test.

Practice task: propose a sampling plan for a fictional layered shore tank and a flowing line. State what each bottle represents and what cannot be concluded from it.

Practice case: a tank may be layered

A fictional tank has 9.0 m of product above a measurable water layer. A single surface sample cannot establish the quality of the whole liquid column. If the nomination requires upper, middle and lower spot samples, calculate the target positions from the verified product depth using the agreed method, then record each actual depth. Keep samples separate until an authorised composite method states how to combine them.

Practice case: transfer starts through a pipeline. A sample taken from a stagnant tap before flow may reflect residue in the branch rather than the new cargo. Record the tap condition and line state and use the approved flushing and sampling plan. For a volatile product, use a method and container designed to limit component loss. State exactly what each sample represents.

Sample handling, labelling and custody

Protect the sample after collection

The quality of a sample depends on everything that happens between collection and analysis. Select a compatible, clean container with the required closure and headspace. Follow the applicable method for volatile, hazardous or temperature-sensitive materials. Close promptly and protect the sample from contamination, evaporation, sunlight, breakage and mixing with another grade.

Label the container at the point of collection with a unique identifier, product, source, sampling method, date, time, sampler and requested analysis. Seal it where required and record the seal number. A label should make sense without relying on somebody's memory. If a correction is needed, preserve the original entry and document who made the change and why.

Maintain a custody log for every handover: sender, receiver, time, condition, seal and destination. Pack samples according to the product hazards and carrier rules; confirm the SDS and transport information before dispatch. Never place incompatible samples together merely because they are going to the same laboratory.

JDL practice: reconcile the field notes, sample register, seals and laboratory receipt. Any broken seal, missing time or unexplained delay is an exception to report, not a detail to conceal.

From bottle to defensible result

Handling begins before the first sample is collected. Prepare an inventory of clean, compatible bottles, labels, seals, absorbent or protective packaging, and a custody register. Verify requested tests and preservation needs with the laboratory. Sample volume and headspace depend on the method; a single fill rule cannot serve every product.

At collection, reconcile bottle ID, tank or line, depth or point, method, product, time and sampler against the field log. Seal where required, then record the seal before the sample leaves the inspector. If one sample is split for parties, identify each split and whether the preparation could affect analysis. Never relabel a bottle from memory after leaving the site.

Transportation is another controlled step. Use compatible packaging and current dangerous-goods requirements where applicable. Record box count, condition, dispatch time, carrier and receipt. A clean sample result does not repair a missing handover. The report should identify compromised samples and avoid unconditional quality claims from them.

Practice task: build a custody chain for six fictional bottles, including one retained counter-sample. At laboratory receipt, one bottle is warm and a second seal is broken. Explain which tests may be affected and how each exception should be reported.

Practice case: three bottles, one missing seal

Three fictional gasoline bottles are labelled with tank, sample depth and collection time. At laboratory receipt, one seal number differs from the field register. Preserve both records and the bottle condition, notify the receiving party and qualify the affected result. Do not retrospectively edit the field register to make the numbers match.

Custody exercise: write a handover record from inspector to courier to laboratory that contains names, times, seal condition and container count. Ask what evidence is missing if a bottle arrives correctly labelled but without an intermediate receipt.

Shore-tank gauging

Establish the tank reference

A tank gauge is meaningful only when the tank, reference datum and measurement point are known. Verify tank identity, calibration table, reference height, gauge point and instrument condition. Check that the liquid is settled as required and note transfer, roof and valve conditions. Confirm safe access and vapour controls before opening a gauge hatch.

Innage measures the distance from the datum to the liquid surface; outage measures from the reference point down to that surface. Select the method specified for the tank and use the matching calibration table. Repeat readings as the governing procedure requires. Record the raw tape or electronic observation, units, reference height and any disagreement, rather than entering only a converted volume.

Measure free water separately using the approved detection method. Floating roofs can change the effective volume; identify the roof position and whether it lies in a critical zone before applying a table or correction. A change in reference height, sediment, roof movement or a mismatched table can produce a convincing but false quantity.

JDL practice: trace tank identity to table revision, raw level, water level, temperature and the person who observed each reading. If references do not reconcile, pause the calculation and investigate.

Static shore-tank measurement

Know the terms before using a table: datum is the zero reference; reference gauge point is the fixed point from which outage is observed; reference height links that point to the datum. Innage is liquid height from the datum; outage is the unfilled distance down from the reference point. The instrument may be a manual tape or approved electronic gauge. Confirm its identity, condition, graduations, calibration status and compatibility with the measurement method.

Take a gauge at the authorised point under site vapour and access controls. Verify tank identity and reference height, note whether the product is moving, and record repeated raw readings under the governing method. If manual and electronic gauges disagree, investigate reference points, instrument status and operating conditions before selecting a result. Convert the accepted gauge using that tank's current calibration table; keep the table version in the audit trail.

Free water is measured separately because it is part of TOV but not product GOV. Confirm detection method and whether the water interface is distinct. Floating-roof position can affect the effective volume and may be particularly uncertain in a critical zone. Document roof status and apply the tank-specific table instructions rather than inventing a roof correction.

Practice task: given a fictional reference height of 12.000 m and two outages of 3.250 m and 3.260 m, calculate both implied innages. The 0.010 m difference is not resolved by averaging automatically; state what checks or repeat readings the method requires before volume lookup.

Worked shore-gauge exercise

Fictional Tank S-4 has a verified reference height of 12.000 m. An outage reading is 3.250 m. The implied innage is 12.000 − 3.250 = 8.750 m, provided both readings use the same datum and units. Check the result against a direct innage reading before looking up volume in the approved S-4 table. A table for S-5 cannot be substituted, even if the tanks have similar dimensions.

A separate water cut is 0.080 m. Record it independently, then obtain the corresponding free-water volume from the correct table or method. If a floating roof is near its critical zone, document roof condition and apply only the tank-specific instruction. The level-to-volume conversion cannot be completed from the level alone because the calibrated table has not been supplied in this exercise.

Marine-vessel tank measurement

Read the vessel as it is

Identify each cargo tank, its approved table, reference point and observed condition. Confirm whether tanks are full, partly filled or apparently empty and whether transfer or ballast operations affect the readings. Record vessel trim and list at the time of measurement. Marine tables and correction instructions are vessel-specific; never carry a shore-tank assumption across to a ship.

Take cargo and free-water gauges consistently with the approved method. Trim and list can move the liquid surface relative to the gauge point, so apply the appropriate corrections before converting to volume. Keep observed and corrected figures separate. For on-board quantity before loading and remaining on board after discharge, inspect and record each relevant tank and line condition; a nominally empty tank may still contain measurable material.

Disputes often arise when tank status, water cuts, trim, list or unmeasured residues are treated differently by two parties. Reconcile the times and inputs before comparing totals. Document inaccessible tanks, equipment failures or conditions that prevent a reliable observation.

JDL practice: build a tank-by-tank record with raw cargo gauge, water gauge, trim, list, correction source, temperature and calculated volume. The total is only as dependable as its least supported tank entry.

Ship measurements and reconciliation

A vessel has multiple tanks, different reference points and tables, and a changing attitude in water. Confirm tank identity, opening and gauge point, whether the tank is full or partly full, and the current trim and list. Record cargo level and water level as raw observations. The vessel's approved table and correction instructions determine how these become volume; shore-tank rules cannot be assumed.

Trim changes the fore-to-aft liquid surface relative to the gauge point; list changes the side-to-side surface. Apply the specified correction for the tank and gauge location, then preserve both observed and corrected levels. For water, a shallow layer may need a different correction or table treatment from the cargo. Record the correction source and any tank for which it is unavailable.

OBQ is material on board before loading; ROB is material left after discharge. Inspect relevant tanks and, where instructed, connected lines. Describe whether residue is liquid, non-liquid or unmeasurable under the applicable method. An estimated zero should not be reported as a measured zero.

Practice task: produce a tank-by-tank worksheet for a fictional vessel with three cargo tanks. Include observed cargo and water levels, trim, list, corrected levels, table volumes and exceptions. Reconcile a discrepancy by reviewing the individual tanks rather than adjusting the grand total.

Worked marine-record exercise

Fictional Vessel V-2 reports Tank 3 cargo outage of 1.620 m, water outage of 8.940 m, trim of 0.6 m by stern and list of 0.4° to port. Record all four observations and the gauge time. Temperature determination

Temperature is a measured input

Petroleum volume changes with temperature, so an unsupported temperature can spoil an otherwise careful gauge. Confirm the thermometer type, identification, range, calibration or verification status and suitability for the cargo. Liquid-in-glass and portable electronic devices have different handling and response characteristics. Follow the approved instrument instructions rather than assuming an instant reading is stable.

Determine how many readings and depths are needed for a representative tank temperature. Lower the instrument safely, allow the required immersion or stabilisation time, and record each actual reading with its depth, tank, time and units. Consider stratification, recent movement and whether the level is sufficient for the planned depths. Do not replace an unusual reading with an expected value; repeat or investigate it and retain both observations.

Use the required averaging and rounding rule only after the raw values have been recorded. Keep cargo temperature distinct from shell temperature or an ambient estimate used for a separate correction. The same temperature record should be traceable through the later volume calculation.

JDL practice: a defensible result states the instrument, verification, reading locations, stabilisation and method used to obtain the reported average.

Instrument verification and representative reading

Liquid-in-glass thermometers and portable electronic instruments require different checks, handling and stabilisation. Before use, confirm calibration or verification evidence, measurement range, resolution, protective condition and units. A damaged instrument or an expired check is not made valid by a plausible display.

Choose depths and reading count from the governing method and actual tank depth. Temperature can vary after heating, circulation, blending or a long settling period. Allow immersion or electronic stabilisation as specified, then record each reading, location, time and instrument. If readings differ materially, investigate whether the tank is stratified and whether a simple average is appropriate.

A reported temperature is an input to the correction chain, not a decorative line on a form. Check whether the calculation uses observed liquid temperature, tank-shell temperature or a separate ambient observation. Record the source of each; do not substitute one for another. Keep the original precision until the governing rounding step.

Practice task: three fictional readings are 27.2°C, 27.8°C and 28.0°C. Compute the unweighted mean, then explain why an approved method might require different depth weighting. If the middle reading was taken during circulation, identify the extra evidence needed before final reporting.

Worked temperature exercise

Fictional Tank S-4 has stable readings of 27.2°C, 27.8°C and 28.0°C at approved depths. If the agreed method calls for an unweighted arithmetic mean, the illustrative result is (27.2 + 27.8 + 28.0) ÷ 3 = 27.666…°C, rounded only by the specified rule. If the method requires depth weighting, this calculation is not valid. Record all three readings and locations before selecting the reporting basis.

A fourth reading of 34.0°C taken during a recirculation event should not be quietly deleted. Keep its time and event, investigate whether it belongs to the same measurement condition, then state the basis used for the final temperature.

Pipeline fullness and transfer conditions

Account for the line between measurement points

A shore-to-vessel difference may reflect a change in the material held in pipelines rather than a measurement error. Identify the route, line capacity, valve state, product interface and whether the line was full at the beginning and end of transfer. Agree a verification method with the responsible parties before the movement where possible.

Approved approaches may include documented circulation, displacement with a known material, controlled high-point checks, pigging or line-pressure evidence. Each has limits and safety requirements. A pressure indication alone does not prove that every part of a line contains the same liquid; high points, gas pockets and interfaces matter. Avoid opening or bleeding a line without site authorisation and controls.

Record who performed the check, when it occurred, the line configuration, measured or observed evidence and the agreed tolerance. If the beginning and ending states differ, show the estimated effect separately from tank measurements and state the uncertainty.

JDL practice: do not force shore and vessel figures to match by assigning an unexplained difference to pipeline fullness. Establish evidence for the line condition first.

Line condition and the cargo balance

A pipeline can retain product, gas or an interface between grades. A change in line inventory between start and finish can appear as a shore-vessel quantity difference. Map the route, branches, high points, valves, capacity and product at each end. Ask whether the line is static, circulating, displaced or pressurised and what each observation actually proves.

Circulation may help establish a uniform product path but needs a documented loop and operating conditions. Displacement introduces a known fluid or product, with an interface that must be tracked. A high-point check can identify gas at a local point but may not represent the whole route. Pigging provides a physical movement indication yet depends on pig travel and recovered material. Line pack or pressure evidence is indirect and must be interpreted with temperature and route geometry. The terminal selects and controls the safe method.

Agree a tolerance and evidence basis before transfer, record beginning and ending states, and quantify the possible inventory change only when supported. Show a line adjustment separately from tank measurements. If the method cannot verify fullness, state that uncertainty instead of declaring the line full.

Practice task: a fictional line holds 18 m³ full and a supported end-state estimate indicates 5 m³ void. Explain how this might affect comparison, and what measurements would be needed before applying an actual quantity adjustment.

Quantity calculation and audit trail

Calculate in a visible sequence

Start with observed data: tank identity, raw gauge, water cut, temperature, trim or list and the relevant table. Convert the corrected level to total observed volume (TOV). Determine free-water volume separately, then gross observed volume (GOV) as the product volume at observed conditions. Apply only the corrections required for that tank, such as steel-shell or floating-roof effects, using approved references.

Apply the correct volume correction factor to obtain gross standard volume (GSV). If the contract requires a net standard volume (NSV), apply the specified sediment-and-water basis. Apparent mass or weight in air requires the appropriate density and conversion basis. Symbols, units, reference temperature and rounding must be consistent throughout. Do not mix an observed volume in one unit with a correction factor or density defined for another basis.

Work one tank at a time and retain intermediate figures before adding totals. Show each source table or factor, the calculation order and the rounding rule. Recalculate a sample tank independently and compare the final shore and vessel numbers only after both chains have been checked.

JDL practice: the report should let a reviewer reproduce the quantity from field notes. A spreadsheet output without raw inputs, table references and correction basis is not an audit trail.

Calculation structure and worked checks

Use a worksheet with two columns: observed data and calculated data. Observed data include raw gauges, free-water cuts, temperatures, trim/list, density or test result and operating times. Calculated data include corrected levels, table volumes, shell or roof effects where applicable, GOV, VCF, GSV, S&W adjustment, NSV and apparent mass. Label every factor with its source, basis and units.

For shore tanks, begin with the calibrated table's TOV and deduct the table-derived free-water volume to find product GOV. Apply any required steel-shell or floating-roof adjustment in the order specified by the approved method. For a ship, correct levels for trim and list as required, use the vessel tables tank by tank, and derive GOV after the separate water treatment. Then apply the correct volume correction factor to obtain GSV. The S&W adjustment gives NSV only on the contract's specified basis. A weight conversion factor or density converts volume to apparent mass only when units and reference conditions are compatible.

Use decimal places and rounding as the agreed method requires. Premature rounding can change totals. Recompute one tank independently and check dimensional consistency: metres cannot be subtracted from cubic metres; a factor for litres cannot silently be used with barrels. Report the calculation sequence so a reviewer can rebuild it from raw notes.

Conversion practice: 1 m³ equals 1,000 L. A fictional GOV of 1,240.00 m³ is 1,240,000 L. Converting units does not change the physical quantity; changing reference temperature or density basis can. Define innage, outage, TOV, GOV, GSV, NSV, VCF, free water, trim, list, OBQ and ROB in your own words before completing the final assessment.

A conversion table is usable only when its source units, target units, reference conditions and precision match the contract.

Chemical cargo inspection and wall-wash testing

Protect cargo quality and personal safety

Chemical inspection can demand tighter control of contamination, compatibility and previous-cargo residues than a routine petroleum movement. Confirm the cargo identity, specification, cleaning standard, coating compatibility, required tests and approved sampling materials. A sample container or hose suitable for one product can be unsuitable for another. Review the SDS and site controls for exposure, reaction and transport hazards.

Tank inspection and wall-wash testing are separate decisions. A visual inspection may identify residue, damage or moisture, but it does not establish every chemical cleanliness criterion. A wall wash uses an agreed solvent and test method to evaluate the accessible surface for specified contaminants. Confirm the test location, solvent purity, sample container, blank, handling and laboratory method. Follow the approved procedure and avoid treating a local passing result as proof of every unseen surface.

Compare findings with the nomination's acceptance criteria. Record tanks tested, areas reached, exceptions, witnesses and any limitation caused by access or safety. Cargo-line condition and sample transport may also affect quality. Where results are ambiguous, request competent review before declaring a tank fit.

JDL practice: report what was inspected and tested, the method and its limits, and the exact criterion used for acceptance. Never issue a blanket cleanliness statement from incomplete evidence.

Chemical inspection and wall-wash decisions

Product and chemical inspections share measurement and custody principles, but chemical cargoes can have stricter contamination and compatibility limits. Confirm the exact specification, previous cargo, tank coating, cleaning evidence, line condition and authorised test plan. Quality field tests and tank observation are distinct forms of evidence. A test result outside specification needs clear identity and sample custody before the inspector draws a conclusion.

Wall wash is a defined test of selected tank surfaces using an agreed solvent and collection method. Select accessible areas under a safe entry or external test plan, use clean compatible equipment, identify solvent batch and blanks, collect and label each wash, and preserve the method's required handling. Funnel collection and filter-paper or blotter observations may serve different test aims; neither is a universal cleanliness certificate.

Record the tank areas inspected, areas not reached, previous-cargo concerns, laboratory method and acceptance criteria. Consider cargo pipelines and hoses separately because a clean tank can still receive contaminated cargo through a dirty line. A water-white chemical may reveal trace colour or haze, but appearance alone is not every specified laboratory test.

Integrated Level 1 assessment

Put the complete workflow together

A reliable inspection begins with a clear nomination and safe work plan. Verify cargo identity and SDS, site controls, authorisation, equipment and measurement standards. Establish tank and line conditions before transfer. Take raw gauges, water and temperature readings; select samples for the requested tests; label, seal and track every handover.

Calculate tank quantities from traceable observations, approved tables and correction factors. Review trim, list, roof and line-state effects where applicable. Compare shore and vessel figures without hiding differences. Investigate anomalies using times, readings, equipment checks, calibration references, operating events and sample records. Report the result, exceptions and uncertainty promptly.

Scenario: a shore tank has a changed reference height, the vessel is trimmed, and the pipeline was partly drained after transfer. A valid report does not simply choose one party's figure. It verifies the tank reference, applies the approved vessel correction, documents the line change and explains which comparison remains uncertain.

Scenario: a chemical sample arrives with a broken seal and an apparently acceptable laboratory result. The result may still be informative, but its chain of custody is compromised. Record the exception and seek direction on resampling or qualification before making a definitive quality claim.

JDL practice: competence means knowing when to measure, when to stop, when to seek specialist review and how to leave an honest record that another inspector can audit.
